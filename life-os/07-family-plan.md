## The strategy, stated plainly

Education and living costs in Indonesia are a fraction of US costs — medical college
especially. On one salary, however high, four kids through US school and university is
not affordable. Jakarta is.

**This is a sound arbitrage, not indecision.** It stays in the system as a *plan*.

The sequence, however, is everything.

## ⚠️ The correction that changes the plan

> **There is no "educational green card." A green card does not permit living abroad
> to study.**

- More than **1 year** abroad without a re-entry permit → generally presumed abandoned
- Even **6+ months** can draw scrutiny at the border
- A **re-entry permit (Form I-131)** buys up to 2 years; renewals are scrutinized

A six-year medical program in Jakarta, held on a green card, would very likely end with
that green card gone — discovered years later, at an airport counter, with no remedy.

## ✅ The right order

> **Come here first → secure permanent status → *then* go abroad for cheap school.**

Once someone is a **US citizen**, living abroad costs them nothing. Citizenship cannot
be abandoned; a green card can. Get status permanent *first*, and Jakarta medical
college becomes pure upside — cheap education with nothing at risk. Exactly the path
your daughter already walked.

Reverse the order — stay in Jakarta now, immigrate later — and each child loses the
easy immigration category at 21, and you pay more for less.

### Where the real clock now sits — naturalization, not the visa

The binding constraint moved. It's no longer "will they get in." It's **"how do they
become citizens before they leave for medical college."**

Because if they hold green cards and go study in Jakarta, residence is at risk. If they
hold **citizenship**, it isn't. So the question is how fast each one can naturalize.

- Your **eldest** arrives around 17. If stepchildren must naturalize on their own after
  ~5 years as a green card holder, that's roughly **age 22** — meaning he'd need to be
  in the US through what would otherwise be his medical-college years. **He is the
  hard case.**
- Your **wife** may become eligible to naturalize a few years after arriving. If she
  does so while the younger children are still minors, living with her in the US, there
  may be a path for them to acquire citizenship through her. That could cover the
  younger three and not the eldest.

This is precisely the scenario to put in front of your lawyer — it's the difference
between one child's plan working and not.

### The wrinkle — they're stepchildren

Stepchildren generally do **not** automatically derive US citizenship through a
stepparent, the way biological or adopted children can. Possible routes: your wife
naturalizing while they are still minors, adoption, or each child naturalizing
individually after holding a green card.

**This is genuinely lawyer territory.** Not a spreadsheet problem.

### The question your lawyer has probably not been asked

An immigration attorney answers what they're asked. If the engagement was *"file I-130s
for my wife and four stepchildren,"* that's what they did — correctly.

**They may have no idea you intend for the kids to live and study in Jakarta after
receiving green cards.** That intention isn't visible anywhere on an I-130. It only
surfaces if you say it out loud.

So the action isn't "find a lawyer" — it's **one specific message to the lawyer you
already have**:

> "After the kids get their green cards, the plan is for them to remain in Jakarta for
> school, including medical college. Does that put their permanent residence at risk?
> What do we need to do to protect it — re-entry permits, timing of entry, or a faster
> route to citizenship? They're my stepchildren, not biological or adopted."

That's the whole thing. It's a five-minute email, and the answer determines whether the
family strategy works or quietly collapses in six years.

### Why it matters this much

Everything in this plan depends on the kids being able to be *cheap in Jakarta* and
*permanent in the US* at the same time. Those two things are in tension, and the
tension is invisible until someone with the right expertise is explicitly pointed at it.

Ask that question. Then the rest of these:

1. Given the petitions are filed and I'm the petitioner — what's each child's category,
   and what's the realistic timeline for each?
2. My 15-year-old ages out first. What protects him? Does CSPA apply here?
3. If they get green cards and then study in Indonesia — how do we avoid abandonment?
   Do re-entry permits actually cover a multi-year degree?
4. As **stepchildren**, what's each one's fastest route to citizenship? Does my wife
   naturalizing while they're minors change it? Does adoption?
5. What's the smartest **arrival order and timing** for wife and four kids?

## You have roughly 18–24 months. Use them.

This is the most valuable thing the lawyer's timeline gives you: **runway.**

Arrival lands around 2028. That is not far away, and it is not a crisis either — it's
exactly enough time to prepare properly, *if you start while it still feels early.*

What that window is for:

| Do now (2026) | Do by 2027 | Do by arrival |
|---|---|---|

The trap is treating 2028 as far away, doing nothing for eighteen months, and then
compressing housing, schooling, budget, and a household of six into a panicked
final quarter. That's the version where something real gets broken.

The whole point of writing any of this down is that **"next year" quietly becomes
"next month."** You've now got a date. Work backward from it.

### One planning question the timeline raises

You mentioned wanting a **6–9 month gap** — wife first, kids after the Jakarta school
year ends. Worth confirming with the lawyer whether that's actually available. Immediate
relatives filed together often move through consular processing **together**, and visas
come with entry deadlines. A staggered arrival may be a choice you have, or it may not
be. Better to know now than to discover it when the visas are issued.
